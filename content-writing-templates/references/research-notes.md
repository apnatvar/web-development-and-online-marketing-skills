# Research notes: reusable content structures

Research snapshot: 2026-10-27. This file captures guidance to inform a template-first content-writing skill. The structures below are synthesized frameworks, not copied post examples. Treat platform advice as orientation, not a guarantee of distribution or business results.

## Organization context: Brownsmith Dynamics

Inspected the public homepage: https://brownsmithdynamics.com/ (crawled 2026-10-27).

Relevant needs for template design: Brownsmith Dynamics serves small businesses in India, including startups and family businesses (roughly 1–50 people). Its two complementary service lines are Discovery (websites, SEO, content, and selected channels to attract relevant customers) and BizFlow (systems, workflow automation, practical AI, and business efficiency). Its stated positioning favors clear practical next steps, useful information, fit-for-purpose implementation, human ownership/review, and concrete customer questions. The homepage presents small, helpful reviews and business-specific outlines as entry points. Templates should therefore support useful education, grounded claims, plain language, and a low-pressure relevant enquiry path. This context is intentionally kept in this research file; it is not a universal rule for the skill.

## Instagram

Source inspected: Meta Newsroom, “Introducing Best Practices, an Education Hub for Creators on Instagram” (2024-10-01): https://about.fb.com/news/2024/10/best-practices-education-hub-creators-instagram/

Meta says its Professional Dashboard Best Practices covers creation, engagement, reach, monetization, and guidelines; creation guidance includes attention capture, frequency, Reel length, audio, and hashtags. The guidance is personalized and subject to continued updates. The announcement does not prescribe a universal caption formula.

Reusable structure (editorial synthesis, not a Meta-mandated format):
1. One specific visual or first-frame promise that earns attention.
2. One audience-relevant situation, question, or misconception.
3. A compact sequence of 2–4 useful points, shown visually or in a short demonstration.
4. A concrete takeaway or outcome, with caveats when needed.
5. One natural next action (save/share/comment/visit/contact), chosen to fit the content.

Template prompts: What should a viewer understand in the first moment? What single point is this post about? What can the visual demonstrate rather than merely decorate? What one action is appropriate? Adapt caption, Reel, Story, or carousel to the idea and account; check current in-app guidance and insights rather than assuming fixed ideal lengths or cadence.

## LinkedIn

Sources inspected:
- LinkedIn Help, “Tips for writing articles on LinkedIn”: https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a516913/tips-for-writing-articles-on-linkedin?lang=en
- LinkedIn Marketing Solutions, “What to post” (PDF): https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/lem/business/en/advertise/linkedin-pages/lms-linkedin-page-posting-best-practices-one-pager.pdf

LinkedIn’s article guidance recommends writing within actual experience/expertise, focusing on one topic, allowing a considered opinion, using media to make concrete experience visible, and publishing when there is something valuable to share. The Page-posting one-pager emphasizes useful tips, actionable steps, and avoiding excessive promotion. These are publisher/platform recommendations, not independent causal evidence.

Reusable structure for a professional insight post:
1. Clear observation, question, or specific point of view.
2. Context: who faces this situation and why it matters.
3. One brief example, reasoning chain, or evidence-backed explanation.
4. 2–4 practical steps, decision criteria, or implications.
5. A concise conclusion and an invitation to discuss a relevant question.

For a longer article, expand with a descriptive title, focused opening, ordered headings, evidence/examples, limitations, and a useful conclusion. Keep within the author’s real knowledge; flag assumptions and distinguish experience from general fact.

## X

Sources inspected:
- X Business, “Get your business started with X”: https://business.x.com/en/basics/get-your-business-started-with-x
- X Business, “Build your presence on X” one-sheet (PDF): https://business.x.com/content/dam/business-twitter/resources/x-downloadable-guides/build-your-presence-on-x-one-sheet.pdf

X’s business guidance calls for concise, real, conversational posts; relevant customer topics; media where useful; and interaction through replies. It suggests keeping hashtags to 1–2 if used, avoiding all-caps, and using a clear CTA when appropriate. A downloadable guide also encourages natural conversation, relevant opinions or questions, and responding to customers. As platform-owned guidance, these are practical recommendations and not performance guarantees.

Reusable structure for a single post:
1. One claim, question, useful observation, or timely point.
2. Brief supporting detail or implication (one or two sentences).
3. Optional media, source, or link that adds substance.
4. Optional direct question or next step.

For a thread: state the central question or conclusion first; number only when sequence helps; give each post one step; support factual claims; finish with a synthesis and relevant link/CTA. Avoid splitting content into a thread just to inflate length.

## Blog / web article

Sources inspected:
- Google Search Central, “Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content”: https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content
- Google Search Central, “SEO Starter Guide”: https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide

Google recommends content for an intended audience, grounded in first-hand knowledge or depth, that leaves readers able to accomplish their goal. It calls for readable organization, descriptive headings, unique value, appropriate freshness, and user language in prominent locations. It explicitly rejects a preferred word count and warns against writing primarily to attract search traffic, mass-producing shallow summaries, or making unsupported promises. Meeting guidance does not ensure crawling, indexing, ranking, or traffic.

Reusable article structure:
1. Descriptive title naming the audience problem or task.
2. Short opening: answer the central question or state what the reader will be able to do.
3. Context and scope: who this applies to, assumptions, and boundaries.
4. Ordered sections that explain the decision/process, with examples, evidence, and steps.
5. Trade-offs, common failure modes, or when the advice does not apply.
6. Summary and one useful next action or related resource.
7. Author/source attribution where useful; update when substance changes.

Choose length based on what fully resolves the reader’s task. Include original experience or analysis; do not rewrite a keyword list as an article outline.

## Email

Sources inspected:
- Mailchimp, “Best Practices for Mailchimp Emails”: https://mailchimp.com/help/best-practices-mailchimp-email/
- Mailchimp Content Style Guide, “Writing Email Newsletters”: https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/writing-email-newsletters/

Mailchimp advises relevant, brand-consistent content; subject and preview text that accurately announce the message; and using subscriber response data to inform decisions. Its newsletter style guidance emphasizes a useful top line and reader-oriented newsletter content. These recommendations are shaped by a vendor’s product and should be adapted to consent, audience expectations, applicable rules, and the sender’s actual data.

Reusable email structure:
1. Subject: specific, truthful reason to open.
2. Preview: a complementary detail, not a repeat of the subject.
3. Opening: why this message is relevant now.
4. Main value: one idea, update, answer, or offer; use short paragraphs and concrete detail.
5. One primary CTA, with a clear expectation of what happens next.
6. Optional secondary material only if it supports the main purpose.
7. Sender identity and required subscription/preference controls for marketing mail.

For a 1:1 business email, start with recipient context, state the purpose early, make the request or answer explicit, and close with a clear next step. Do not fabricate familiarity, urgency, results, or personalization. Evaluate subject/content variants with the sender’s own opted-in audience and suitable measures; no generic best-practice source establishes guaranteed opens or conversions.

## Evidence limits and implementation notes

- Platform materials communicate product-owner guidance and can change; revisit current official pages before encoding detailed limits or feature-specific rules.
- The templates are composition scaffolds synthesized from high-level guidance. They are not proven formulas and should remain adaptable to audience, objective, format, and available evidence.
- None of the inspected sources supports guaranteed reach, ranking, engagement, leads, opens, or revenue. Do not turn advice about usefulness, clarity, or testing into outcome promises.
- Keep facts, customer claims, statistics, quotations, credentials, case studies, and calls to action tied to supplied evidence and the user's actual offer. If evidence is missing, use a placeholder or ask for it rather than inventing proof.
- Avoid copying examples or wording from source materials. The structures above are abstracted and newly phrased.
