# A Simple Decision Record for Small Research Teams

## The conclusion is only one part of the decision

A product team can preserve every final choice and still lose the knowledge that made those choices useful.

Consider a fictional research cycle. An interview note contains an observation. A chat thread captures the team's uncertainty. A roadmap records the chosen direction. Months later, the team can see what it did, but reconstructing why means searching across tools and relying on memory.

The failure is gradual. Nothing appears missing at the moment of the decision because everyone involved still remembers the context. The gap becomes visible when evidence changes, a new colleague asks about the tradeoff, or a retrospective needs more than the final answer.

## Use the decision as the unit of organisation

Instead of starting with another folder structure, start with a record for the decision itself. A small version needs four fields.

### 1. Source

Link the research, observation, technical constraint, or document that informed the discussion. Do not copy sensitive material into a public record. Preserve enough provenance for an authorised teammate to understand the source.

### 2. Open question

Write what the team still did not know. This separates the evidence available at the time from hindsight. It also makes clear which uncertainty might justify revisiting the decision.

### 3. Conclusion

State the choice in plain language. Include the rejected alternative only when it helps explain the tradeoff; avoid turning the record into a transcript of every opinion.

### 4. Owner

Name who will revisit the decision when new evidence arrives. Ownership is not proof that the record will stay current, but an owner makes the maintenance expectation visible.

## Decide what deserves a record

The method has a cost. If a team creates a formal record for every small choice, the records become another neglected archive. A useful threshold might include decisions that are expensive to reverse, depend on uncertain evidence, affect several functions, or are likely to be questioned later.

The exact threshold is a team decision. The important part is to define it before the repository fills with noise.

## Keep the record close to the work

The four fields can live in a document, wiki, issue tracker, or database. A team should first test the method in a tool it already uses. That exposes the behavioral questions before a software choice obscures them:

- Who creates the record?
- When is it considered complete?
- What event should trigger a review?
- Who can see the underlying sources?
- When should the record be archived?

No tool resolves those questions automatically.

## Where the fictional product fits

LumenDesk is a fictional beta for small research teams. Its stated structure includes decision spaces, source cards, and shared conclusions. The fictional positioning is that research should be organised around decisions rather than document folders.

That description is not independent evidence of faster research, better decisions, or reduced work. This example has no customers, testimonials, or performance results. It demonstrates how a campaign can distinguish a product mechanism from an unsupported outcome claim.

## Try the method before the product

Create one record for a recent decision your team may need to revisit. Add the source, open question, conclusion, and owner. Then ask whether the record made the reasoning easier to inspect and whether maintaining it felt proportionate.
