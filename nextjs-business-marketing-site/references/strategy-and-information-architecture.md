# Strategy and information architecture

Help a business website make its purpose, audience, offers, evidence and next steps understandable. Treat the company’s chosen positioning as an input to execute, not an invitation to replace it. Research can expose ambiguity or risk; it does not by itself authorize a strategic pivot.

Classify recommendations as required correctness or publication behavior, conditional changes that need evidence, or optional voice and presentation choices. Keep the categories visible in plans.

## Establish the working brief

Before proposing pages or rewriting copy, establish:

- What outcome does the business help a customer reach, and which offers are actually available?
- Who buys and uses the work, where is it offered, and what constraints influence a decision?
- What proof exists: delivered work, measured outcomes, customer evidence, demonstrations, independent sources, or only hypotheses?
- Which positioning choices are settled, which are being tested, and who can change them?
- What useful action should the visitor be able to take next?

Use supplied strategy and verified first-party material first. Research customers when a decision depends on their needs or language. Competitor pages show competitor claims, terminology and visible page patterns; they do not establish demand, performance, quality or what this business should promise. Search trends, keyword estimates and impressions have different scopes. Do not recast them as qualified demand or revenue.

Separate user-approved strategy, customer evidence, first-party delivery evidence, independent published evidence, competitor claims, platform diagnostics and hypotheses. Keep those categories clear in planning and public claims. Never turn activity measures such as commits, impressions or page views into customer outcomes.

When positioning is under an agreed observation period, preserve the core message long enough to interpret evidence. Correct factual, accessibility, usability and functional problems as needed. Keep experiments narrow enough to learn from: change a substantive audience, question, example or offer framing, and record the change. Avoid near-identical pages that only swap a phrase. A review date is a checkpoint for deliberate discussion, not an automatic strategy reversal. Dates, duration and experiment scope are business-specific choices.

## Organize around buyer decisions

Give each primary offer a clear outcome and plain-language explanation. A business may have one offer or several complementary paths; do not impose another company’s architecture. Place capabilities beneath the problem they solve. A useful page may move from visitor need to outcome, approach, service and technology, or follow another order that better fits the query, audience and evidence. Specialist pages may lead with a solution when the intent calls for it.

A useful prioritization map is commercial pages, proof pages, durable authority content, useful tools/resources and experiments. Treat this as a decision aid, not a required page inventory. Experiments and utility traffic should not obscure the offers the business sells. Product categories should help visitors choose among real options. Make a category or detail page only when it serves a distinct task, adds useful information and improves the decision.

For important pages, keep a compact intent record: intended reader, problem or query, page purpose, offer or next step, evidence, related pages and indexation state. Use query evidence where available; label inferred intent as a hypothesis. Keyword fields are planning labels, not proof of demand or a reason to force phrases into copy.

## Decide whether a page earns a place

Before proposing a new indexable page, ask whether its audience and intent are materially distinct, whether the business can answer credibly, whether it helps a visitor decide or complete a task, and whether merging it into an existing page would make that page worse. Also consider whether it can be maintained and linked from relevant contexts.

Improve an existing page when it already serves substantially the same intent. Avoid thin keyword variants, doorway pages, bulk location/service permutations without distinct local value, shallow comparisons, duplicate services, mass-generated pages and repetitive FAQs. Publish a regional page only when the business genuinely serves that place and can provide useful distinct information, such as verified service availability, local process or constraints, relevant staff or facilities, locally applicable pricing and terms, or original evidence that helps a local visitor decide. A city name swapped into a template is not distinct value. A filter or search result can be useful without becoming an indexable landing page. Usually index the useful parent collection and keep weak permutations out of the index. These defaults apply to proposed pages; they do not authorize changing the status of existing pages.

Protect pages that already attract relevant visitors or support the business. Before major rewriting or consolidation, inspect query fit, available analytics, links and business value. If data are unavailable, say so and prefer a smaller reversible improvement. Page count, raw traffic and rankings are not business outcomes on their own.

## Connect information to a next step

Link useful information to commercial relevance, proof and contact. Connect an article or tool to an offer only where the link helps the reader. Give visitors a low-friction next action appropriate to readiness: compare an offer, request a scoped conversation or try a working tool. A CTA must describe something the business can deliver. Do not claim a free audit, guarantee, fixed price or assessment unless the experience supports it.

A clear primary CTA may help when the page has one decision. Add secondary actions when they serve distinct needs. Place invitations where they help the visitor rather than following a fixed count. Keep price, exclusions, provider costs and scope near the choice they explain.

## Lessons from iteration

A broad technology catalogue can obscure the customer outcome; organizing around buyer needs often makes choices clearer. That does not mean every business needs the same offer structure. Metadata and public descriptions should agree, but consistency alone does not demonstrate conversion or ranking gains. Old route removals and prior strategy decisions describe the past; they do not authorize future changes.
