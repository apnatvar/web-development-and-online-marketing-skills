# Main-site copywriting

Write copy that helps visitors recognize a need, understand what the business does, judge whether it fits and choose a useful next step. Start from the owner’s approved positioning and available evidence. Do not substitute a trendy technology, broad catalogue or generic agency label for the company’s actual purpose.

Treat factual accuracy and honest scope as requirements. Treat page order, voice and CTA prominence as choices to fit the brief, visitor task and evidence.

## Build the message around the buyer

For each page, identify the reader, the job they are trying to do, what gets in the way, the relevant offer and what action makes sense. One useful sequence is:

1. **Problem or outcome.** Use language the reader recognizes and say why it matters.
2. **Approach.** Describe what the business changes, simplifies or helps the customer decide.
3. **Offer.** Make scope and fit concrete with deliverables, steps, examples or a demonstration.
4. **Technology.** Name it when it helps the reader understand the work, choose an offer or assess a real limitation.
5. **Evidence and next step.** Show what is known, qualify what is uncertain and offer a real action.

A homepage should make the company, audience, primary outcome and main choices clear quickly. A service page should let a visitor assess fit before requiring them to understand technical detail. A product page should explain the problem, capabilities, operating assumptions, limits, price and support where relevant. A category page should help visitors compare genuine options instead of repeating every product description. Pair unfamiliar service or product names with a plain explanation.

A business may have one offer or several related offers. Describe their relationship only when it helps the buyer. Do not impose another company’s pillar structure or let internal taxonomy stand in for customer language.

## Write with clarity and evidence

Prefer familiar words, active verbs, short sentences and small paragraphs. Use terms such as AI, API, automation, analytics or SEO when they accurately describe the work and matter to the reader. Explain implementation details when they affect scope, ownership, risk, cost or choice. Translate features into mechanisms and outcomes; a tool is not valuable solely because it is new.

Keep claims proportionate to evidence. Distinguish delivered work, measured outcomes, independent research, internal observations, hypotheses and proposals. Label forecasts and illustrative examples. Never invent clients, testimonials, savings, rankings, guarantees, benchmarks, expertise or capabilities. Do not imply AI inherently performs people’s work better or that automation removes the need for judgment and ownership. Explain review, exceptions, maintenance and handoff when these affect a decision.

Place proof near the claim it supports. A case study should identify context, problem, constraints, actual work, result and measurement basis. If impact was not measured, describe the delivered change rather than implying business results. Internal engineering activity is not customer proof.

Direct, affirmative phrasing can work well for a plain-language offer. Choose wording that fits the established brand voice and visitor expectations. In any style, keep qualifications close to the claim, avoid burying the offer in defensive copy, and keep internal process notes out of promotional text.

## Structure pages around decisions

Make the page subject clear near the top and arrange sections to answer the reader’s next questions. One H1 is a common convention, not an SEO guarantee; follow the project’s semantic structure. Put a concise answer under a substantive question heading, then expand with mechanism, example, proof and limitations. Use H2s for main questions and H3s for true subdivisions. Keep sections additive: establish each major benefit once, then use later sections for delivery, examples, evidence or next steps.

A commercial page often works well in this order: outcome, audience fit, recognizable problem, approach and scope, example or proof, delivery and ownership, next step. An informational page can start with the answer, then explain distinctions, steps, evidence and limits before connecting to a relevant resource. Adapt the sequence to the user journey; this is not a rigid template. Preserve useful sections and the page’s existing search intent when editing.

Use diagrams, comparisons and tables when they explain a choice faster than prose. Keep text accessible, responsive and understandable without animation. Preserve the site’s design system and mobile hierarchy. Avoid visual components that compete with the main message or repeat it.

## Use FAQs and proof where they help

Add FAQs when visitors need direct answers about scope, fit, process or ownership that the main copy does not already provide. Put answers near the relevant decision where practical. Keep them visible, specific and consistent with the offer. Remove questions that repeat headings, exist only for keyword coverage or imply unsupported capabilities. Do not use a long FAQ section to avoid clarifying the service itself.

Use case studies and testimonials only when authentic and approved for publication. Give evidence enough context to be meaningful, including what changed and how results were measured. Keep external research distinct from company results. If evidence is limited, show a process, demonstration, sample or deliverable honestly rather than borrowing credibility.

## Make calls to action specific

A page may emphasize a primary next step when that matches the visitor task. Use labels that describe real destinations, such as comparing options, viewing a service, trying a tool, asking a question or requesting a conversation. Secondary actions can serve distinct needs. Place invitations where they help the decision; no single CTA count or placement fits every page. Keep reachable contact channels visible and appropriate to the audience.

Place prices, third-party costs, eligibility, exclusions and maintenance terms where they help the decision. Regional indicative prices must state currency, unit, tax treatment and scope. Do not imply live currency conversion unless rates are actually refreshed from a defined source. Do not describe an assessment as free unless it is actually available and scoped. An estimator should follow supported pricing rules and explain inclusions; do not calculate quotes from invented assumptions.


## Lessons from iteration

Outcome-led copy can make a technical business easier to understand when it explains the service in plain language. Reducing repeated calls to action can make the next step clearer, but neither approach guarantees better conversion. Preserve the existing brand voice and check performance before making broad rewrites.
