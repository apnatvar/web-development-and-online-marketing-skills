# Message Strategy and Ethical Persuasion Frameworks

Use this reference when the work includes campaign strategy, positioning, copy, offer presentation, or test design. Do not load it for a purely mechanical MJML correction.

Frameworks organize decisions; they do not prove what an audience wants or guarantee a result. Mark assumptions, support claims with supplied evidence, and test consequential choices. The labels below distinguish research-grounded models from practitioner heuristics.

## Build the message from customer evidence

### Jobs to Be Done — practitioner research lens

Write the customer's situation as:

> When [situation], I want to [progress], so I can [desired outcome].

Then record the obstacle, current alternative, selection criteria, anxiety, and evidence available. Use this to decide what the email should help the recipient do. Do not turn demographic stereotypes or inferred private traits into a "job."

### Positioning statement — practitioner strategy tool

Define:

> For [specific audience and situation], [offer] is the [credible category] that [relevant benefit], because [verifiable difference or proof].

Use the statement as an internal constraint, not as finished copy. If the category, difference, or proof is unclear, state the uncertainty instead of inventing differentiation.

### Benefit ladder and message house — practitioner tools

Translate each important feature through:

`feature -> functional effect -> customer outcome -> emotional or social meaning`

Stop at the highest level supported by evidence. Organize the result into one central promise, two or three supporting reasons, and proof for each reason. Remove any pillar that cannot be substantiated.

## Choose how much persuasion the message can support

### Elaboration Likelihood Model — research-grounded model

Use it as a hypothesis about attention and involvement:

- For high-stakes, expensive, unfamiliar, or comparison-heavy decisions, provide concrete arguments, terms, demonstrations, limitations, and evidence.
- For low-attention reminders, use recognizable branding, one clear benefit, and a low-effort action, while keeping all material conditions available.

Do not use surface cues, celebrities, polish, or authority badges to conceal weak evidence. A short email may link to detailed proof; brevity does not justify omission.

### Self-Determination Theory — research-grounded model

When relevant, support:

- **autonomy:** real options, preference controls, and non-coercive language;
- **competence:** understandable steps, examples, and progress feedback;
- **relatedness:** a credible human sender and an appropriate sense of belonging.

This is often more durable than pressure. Never imply that a recipient is inadequate for declining the offer.

### Regulatory focus — research-grounded model

Test either a promotion frame (growth, gain, advancement) or a prevention frame (security, reliability, avoiding a credible problem) when it matches the real customer goal. Do not turn a prevention frame into fearmongering or exaggerate loss.

## Select a copy structure

These are practitioner heuristics, not validated descriptions of a universal buying process. Choose the smallest structure that fits the message.

| Situation | Useful structure | Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short promotion or announcement | **AIDA** | Attention -> relevant interest -> supported desire -> action |
| Clear, costly problem | **PAS** | Problem -> consequences without exaggeration -> solution |
| Transformation with credible contrast | **BAB** | Before -> achievable after -> bridge |
| Product or feature explanation | **FAB** | Feature -> practical advantage -> recipient benefit |
| Evidence-sensitive offer | **Problem-Proof-Plan-Action** | Need -> substantiation -> next steps -> CTA |
| Editorial email | **APP** | Agree on context -> promise useful insight -> preview the substance |
| Transactional message | **Status-Details-Next step-Support** | Put operational truth before any secondary content |

Apply the **Rule of One** where possible: one principal reader, problem, promise, and action. Complex updates may require multiple tasks; do not force them into a promotional funnel.

Before finalizing, use the **4Cs** as an editing check: clear, concise, compelling, credible. “Compelling” never overrides “credible.”

## Use stories and proof deliberately

Narrative transportation is a research-grounded account of how engagement with a story can influence attitudes. Use a brief customer situation, tension, action, and result only when it clarifies the use case. Label composites and hypotheticals; obtain permission for identifiable stories; do not invent testimonials or tidy away material limitations.

Cialdini's influence principles are evidence-informed prompts for review, not a checklist to force into every message:

- use authority only with relevant, current credentials;
- use social proof only when representative and traceable;
- use reciprocity by giving genuine value without manufacturing obligation;
- use consistency through user-chosen commitments, not traps;
- use scarcity only for a real, material limit;
- use liking and unity without pretending intimacy or shared identity.

## Behavioural-economics audit

Treat these effects as context-dependent hypotheses. Record the proposed mechanism and the evidence needed before using one.

| Effect | Legitimate use | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Framing / reference dependence | Explain the same true outcome from a relevant perspective | Omit a material comparison or alternate frame |
| Anchoring | Show a real list price, benchmark, or plan comparison | Invent a reference price or inflate it before discounting |
| Loss aversion | Explain a concrete consequence of delay or inaction | Threaten vague loss or shame the recipient |
| Defaults / status quo | Preselect a safe, reversible operational choice | Precheck marketing consent or hide opt-out |
| Social proof | Report sourced, representative adoption or results | Fake counts, testimonials, logos, or cherry-picked outcomes |
| Scarcity | State verified inventory, capacity, or expiry | Evergreen countdowns or false “last chance” claims |
| Choice overload | Group options and recommend by stated need | Hide alternatives or make the preferred choice hard to refuse |
| Goal gradient | Show accurate onboarding or completion progress | Manufacture progress to pressure continued action |

Run a **reactance check** before send: remove commands, guilt, surveillance language, forced continuity, and implied penalties that are not real. Make the recipient's freedom to decline clear where appropriate.

## Turn the framework into a testable brief

For each campaign, record only the frameworks that change a decision:

1. customer job and evidence source;
2. positioning statement and message-house proof;
3. chosen copy structure and why it fits;
4. any motivational or behavioural hypothesis;
5. ethical risk and guardrail;
6. observable outcome and guardrail metric.

Test the message hypothesis, not the framework's name. For example: “Showing the three setup steps will increase qualified activations without increasing unsubscribes,” rather than “test competence.” Prefer downstream outcomes over opens, and reject a lift obtained through deception, accidental clicks, complaints, or reduced comprehension.
